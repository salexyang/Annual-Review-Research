How the simulation works

Research output and review levels

Annual publication and R&R counts are independent of each other, independent across years, and independent of good/bad years. R&Rs are events, not a model of individual manuscripts progressing toward publication. The highest qualifying review level wins; publication and R&R counts are not added together. Classification histograms pool all annual assessments within the measured period (replications × years). Their mean and standard deviation describe the numeric review levels 1–5; simulated output before year one does not add assessments.

Comparing Average and Star researchers

The Average researcher uses the entered research parameters. The Star researcher multiplies both publication and R&R means and standard deviations by the selected factor. In each replication, one Average and one Star researcher are drawn independently and share the same good/bad-year sequence. Each type’s exact output history is reused across all policies. The comparison counts every measured year where the Star review level is strictly above the Average review level; ties and Average-higher cases are reported separately. Its denominator is replications × years. The final-salary comparison instead compares annual salary at the end of the horizon, once per pair, with a denominator equal to the number of replications. It uses the same paired histories and reports higher, tied, and lower salaries; numerical round-off is treated as a tie. At a 1× multiplier, researchers have the same distribution but independent histories, so they need not tie.

Rolling totals, reviewed every year

A three-year rule uses this year plus the preceding two years, sums the counts, and moves forward one year at the next review. It never divides by three and never waits three years between raises. Output before year one is simulated to fill the initial window; that history does not generate salary payments. An event can contribute to multiple overlapping windows.

Two salary outcomes

A raise applies at the start of each year. Final salary growth is the compounded percentage increase from starting salary. Total earnings sums the full salary received in every measured year, including base pay. The model excludes inflation, taxes, discounting, and other salary changes.

Yearly salary = previous salary × (1 + raise)
Total earnings = sum of yearly salary

Reading the distributions

Bars show the percentage of simulated histories in each interval. Every policy uses the same intervals and axis limits for a given outcome. Standard deviation measures the spread across simulated outcomes: percentage points (pp) for final salary growth, and dollars for total earnings. The central 80% range runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile; it describes possible outcomes, not uncertainty about the mean.

Both research counts use the Consul–Jain generalized Poisson model. When variance equals the mean, it reduces to ordinary Poisson. For variance below the mean, the finite-support probabilities are normalized and numerically calibrated to match the entered mean and standard deviation. Zero SD with a whole-number mean means fixed output. Inputs that cannot be matched reliably produce an error. See Scollnik (1998) for the normalized formulation. Distribution shape can affect threshold-based results.
